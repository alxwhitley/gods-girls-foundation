### Her story — full narrative, warm-linen
Body copy (verbatim, do not rewrite):

"In 2018, Pam was diagnosed with breast cancer and underwent a single
mastectomy. In recovery, she often went without a prosthesis at all — until
a friend gave her one, hand-knitted by a local group of women. It was the
best gift she'd received. It was also far from perfect.

The wool shrank over time, leaving one side visibly larger than the other.
It was uncomfortable, and more than once Pam found herself slipping away
mid-service to adjust it — a harder thing to hide when she was the one
ministering that day. She became self-conscious, certain people noticed
what she couldn't fix.

She wasn't alone in it. She saw the same struggle in women at church, in
stores, in hospital waiting rooms. She recognized it most painfully in her
own mother, who had faced breast cancer years earlier and used old,
crumpled cloth in place of a real prosthesis — a quiet indignity that went
unspoken but never unnoticed. At the time of her own diagnosis, Pam didn't
know of a single shop that sold proper supplies. She'd heard of one,
eventually, but the prices put it out of reach.

Relief came through a woman she calls a friend to this day: a breast cancer
survivor herself, serving as a missionary in Mombasa. Once a year, this
woman opened her yard and sold prostheses, mastectomy bras, swimwear, and
everything else a survivor might need. She taught Pam what to look for in a
proper fit — soft seams, a wide underband that sits flat against the chest,
deep front and side panels, full cups, wide adjustable straps, no
underwire.

For a few years, Pam bought her supplies from her and learned everything
she could. It changed how she carried herself. She began introducing other
survivors to the same source, and found real joy in watching their
confidence return once they were properly fitted.

Then the missionary's cancer returned, and she passed away. It was a loss
felt widely — the women who had depended on her were left searching for a
supplier that, in Kenya, barely existed. When supplies could be found at
all, they were often unaffordable, and many women were still left making do
with rags — visibly uneven, and impossible to hide.

Pam couldn't unsee the gap that left behind. In 2023, after her own further
treatment, her friend and sister Nneka Hay brought her to a
mastectomy-supply store in Raleigh, North Carolina. Nneka explained the
situation facing women back home in Kenya, and the store owner agreed to
help gather donations.

That was the moment God's Girls Foundation was born."
